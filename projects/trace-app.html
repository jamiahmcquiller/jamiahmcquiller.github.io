Project Overview

TRACE is a team-based academic project exploring how people find, interpret, and share information about crime and safety in their communities. Relevant information may be spread across public records, police websites, news reports, and other sources, making it difficult to locate information in one place.

The project explores a centralized, location-aware interface where users can browse incident information, filter by category and time, inspect incident details, and manage preferences for locations and alerts.

Project objective: Explore how user-centered research and interface design can make public-safety information more accessible, understandable, and relevant to the places people care about.

The prototype may use illustrative data and simulated interactions where live services or backend functionality have not been implemented.

The Problem

People may rely on different sources to learn about crime and safety concerns in their communities. Information can be difficult to compare, may lack context, or may not be easy to filter by location and incident type.

Receiving information also raises important questions: Is it current? Is it verified? Is it relevant to a particular location? Could its presentation create unnecessary fear or unfairly stigmatize a neighborhood?

TRACE explores ways to present incident information with clearer context, location-based navigation, user-selected filters, and explicit distinctions between reported and verified information.

Design Goals

Localized Awareness

Help users explore incident information associated with places that matter to them, such as a campus, workplace, home area, or downtown district.

Relevant Information

Explore category and time filters so users can narrow sample incident listings to information relevant to their interests.

User-Controlled Alerts

Design preferences for incident categories, selected locations, notification radius, and alert behavior.

Clear Incident Context

Present incident summaries, timestamps, approximate locations, and appropriate disclaimers to help users interpret information carefully.

Privacy-Conscious Design

Consider privacy, responsible information sharing, approximate location display, and risks associated with exposing sensitive information.

Usability

Evaluate navigation, action visibility, selected states, feedback, and recovery paths so users can understand what to do and what happened.

User Research

The research explored how people find and use information about crime and safety in their communities. Interviews and surveys were used to investigate user behaviors, information needs, concerns, and limitations of existing sources.

Research Questions

  • How do people learn about incidents in their communities?
  • How do they decide whether safety information is credible?
  • What do people do after witnessing or experiencing a safety concern?
  • What information do users consider urgent versus less time-sensitive?
  • What frustrations arise when information is incomplete, outdated, or difficult to find?
  • How do people use crime information when making everyday decisions?
  • What notification preferences and controls would be useful?

Research Approach

  1. Contextual inquiry: Explore participants' experiences and existing behaviors through open-ended questions and observations.
  2. Research documentation: Record observations, participant statements, frustrations, anxieties, and workarounds without assuming motivations participants did not express.
  3. Pattern identification: Organize research notes into related themes to help identify user needs and design opportunities.
  4. Personas and journeys: Use research evidence to inform representative user profiles and current-state journey maps.

Public research artifacts should be anonymized and shared in accordance with applicable course and participant privacy requirements.

Prototype & Key Features

The design progressed from early conceptual work and low-fidelity prototyping toward a medium-fidelity application prototype. The project explores the following application areas.

Home & Incident Feed

Browse illustrative incident cards using feed concepts such as For You, Recent, or Nearby, and open individual entries for more information.

Interactive Map

Explore an incident map centered on the University of Michigan–Flint and the surrounding downtown Flint test area, with illustrative markers and incident-detail navigation.

Filters

Explore filtering by incident category and time range, with selected states, apply and clear controls, and potential no-results recovery.

Notifications

Explore preferences for selected locations, incident categories, and alert settings.

Saved Locations

Explore location search and saved-location concepts that allow users to select places they want to follow.

Replay Concept

Browse illustrative dispatch-feed entries and simulated playback states as part of the incident-awareness experience.

Incident Details

Present sample incident categories, timestamps, approximate locations, summaries, and limitations.

Sharing

Explore a proposed sharing flow for illustrative incident information while considering privacy and responsible handling of shared content.

Usability Evaluation

The project included a cognitive walkthrough of key user tasks to identify where people might struggle to understand an action, find a control, associate it with the intended outcome, or recognize the result of an interaction.

Examples of Design Opportunities

Area Usability Concern Proposed Improvement
Incident discovery Users may not immediately recognize how to open an incident from the map or feed. Make incident markers and detail actions more visible and clearly interactive.
Location management Users need confirmation that a location has been selected and saved. Show selected states, explicit confirmation, and a visible saved-location entry.
Notification preferences Users may not know whether their selections have been saved. Provide clear save controls and a summary of selected alert preferences.
Incident sharing Users may need clearer feedback while composing or sending a share action. Make input controls and completion feedback more apparent.
Filtering Users need to understand which filters are active and how to reset them. Highlight selected options and provide clear Apply and Clear All actions.
Evaluation takeaway: A control should not only be visible. Users should understand what it does, see the result of their action, and have a way to recover when they select the wrong option or encounter an empty result.

Privacy, Trust & Responsible Design

Crime and public-safety interfaces can influence how people perceive risk and their communities. TRACE considers responsible information presentation an important design requirement.

  • Clearly label illustrative or unverified incident information.
  • Do not imply that a dispatch call automatically confirms that a crime occurred.
  • Avoid unnecessary disclosure of victims' identities, private individuals' details, or precise sensitive locations.
  • Consider approximate or aggregated location display where appropriate.
  • Avoid visualizations that unnecessarily stigmatize neighborhoods or imply incident counts alone explain safety.
  • Do not publish real dispatch recordings, personal data, or private participant information in portfolio materials.
